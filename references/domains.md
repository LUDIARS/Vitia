Each domain is a lens for a marketing problem, not a label for a person.

## 1. Superbia — identity and credible distinction

**Disciplines:** social identity, self-concept, signaling theory, status consumption, brand positioning.

**Use when:** the offer helps customers express mastery, belonging, taste, expertise, autonomy, or earned distinction.

**Algorithm:**

1. Determine whether the category is identity-relevant and whether use is visible to self or others.
2. Name the desired identity in behavioral terms: what the customer can do, demonstrate, or contribute.
3. Choose one route: mastery, belonging, or distinction.
4. Map a verified feature to credible evidence of that identity.
5. Prefer earned status, competence, craft, and contribution over superiority claims.
6. Test identity framing against a functional-benefit control.

**Output:** identity proposition, credible signal, proof artifact, and status-shame guardrail.

**Avoid when:** the purchase is purely private and utilitarian, evidence is weak, or the framing relies on class, race, gender, disability, or humiliation.

**Metrics:** qualified premium acceptance, identity-fit rating, referral quality, trust, and status discomfort.

## 2. Avaritia — value and reference-dependent choice

**Disciplines:** behavioral economics, prospect theory, mental accounting, reference dependence, pricing research.

**Use when:** the buyer faces measurable costs, gains, risks, switching costs, or ownership value.

**Algorithm:**

1. Establish the customer's real reference point and alternative, including doing nothing.
2. Calculate value with disclosed assumptions: total cost, expected benefit range, payback period, and uncertainty.
3. Choose the truthful frame that matches the decision: gain, avoided loss, or cost of delay.
4. Pair every quantified claim with provenance and a boundary condition.
5. Reduce risk with a fair trial, warranty, staged commitment, or reversible choice.
6. Test value framing against the same offer without framing; never change hidden fees between treatments.

**Output:** value equation, reference point, proof, risk reversal, and transparent offer.

**Avoid:** fabricated scarcity, inflated anchors, sunk-cost pressure, misleading savings, or targeting financial distress.

**Metrics:** qualified conversion, realized ROI, refund, payment failure, regret, and complaint rate.

## 3. Luxuria — anticipated experience and desire

**Disciplines:** affective science, sensory marketing, incentive salience, mental simulation, experience design.

**Use when:** value is best understood through an experience, demonstration, sensory quality, emotional tone, or immediate feedback.

**Algorithm:**

1. Identify the concrete experience the customer seeks; do not begin with an abstract emotion word.
2. Translate verified features into a vivid episode using relevant senses, action, context, and timing.
3. Shorten psychological distance with a demo, preview, sample, prototype, or reversible trial.
4. Make the reward legible without claiming that a message “triggers dopamine.”
5. Match intensity to the category and audience; preserve an easy stop.
6. Test vivid experience against factual description while holding the offer constant.

**Output:** experience scene, demonstration, immediate value moment, and intensity cap.

**Avoid:** sexual coercion, body shame, addiction exploitation, deceptive simulation, or unsupported neural claims.

**Metrics:** demo completion, trial-to-value time, qualified desire, conversion, regret, and harmful-use indicators.

## 4. Invidia — comparison and attainable aspiration

**Disciplines:** social comparison theory, reference-group effects, benign and malicious envy, social proof.

**Use when:** customers evaluate progress relative to relevant peers, exemplars, benchmarks, or competing approaches.

**Algorithm:**

1. Identify a genuinely relevant comparison group; similarity must be defensible.
2. Determine whether the comparison is attainable and the exemplar's advantage appears deserved.
3. Use benign-envy routing: show a reachable pathway, actions, and proof rather than a superior person alone.
4. If the comparison evokes resentment or distance, switch from imitation to differentiation or remove it.
5. Show distributions, denominators, and selection criteria for social proof.
6. Test peer-pathway framing against non-comparative progress framing.

**Output:** reference group, comparison basis, pathway, proof, and shame check.

**Avoid:** fake popularity, cherry-picked peers, humiliation, protected-trait comparison, or grievance amplification.

**Metrics:** pathway engagement, qualified conversion, trust, negative sentiment, and perceived attainability.

## 5. Gula — repeat value, learning, and variety

**Disciplines:** reinforcement learning, reward prediction error, habit formation, hedonic adaptation, satiation, variety seeking.

**Use when:** customer value unfolds across repeated sessions, consumption, learning, collections, subscriptions, or content.

**Algorithm:**

1. Map the loop as cue -> voluntary action -> user value -> feedback -> next useful state.
2. Define the user benefit produced on every cycle; reject loops whose primary output is merely more time spent.
3. Set a predictable baseline reward and use novelty to reveal real value, not to manufacture compulsion.
4. Track satiation and introduce meaningful variety, pacing, or rest.
5. Add stopping cues, usage controls, and easy cancellation before optimizing frequency.
6. Test repeat-value design on retention and customer outcome, not engagement alone.

**Output:** value loop, feedback timing, variety plan, satiation response, and stopping rule.

**Avoid:** gambling-like variable rewards, infinite consumption without stops, child-directed compulsion, or calling dopamine a pleasure button.

**Metrics:** value-qualified retention, goal completion, fatigue, overuse, cancellation ease, and long-term satisfaction.

## 6. Ira — blocked goals, justice, and restored agency

**Disciplines:** appraisal theories of emotion, psychological reactance, procedural justice, service recovery, conflict communication.

**Use when:** customers face a blocked goal, unfair process, loss of control, complaint, category frustration, or credible incumbent failure.

**Algorithm:**

1. Diagnose the appraisal: blocked goal, unfairness, blame, uncertainty, or threatened autonomy.
2. Validate the concrete problem with evidence; do not intensify emotion beyond the facts.
3. Name a process or behavior that can be changed, not an identity group or scapegoat.
4. Restore agency with choices, explanation, correction, and a reversible next step.
5. For recovery, prioritize acknowledgment and repair before an upsell.
6. Test autonomy-supportive language against directive language and monitor reactance.

**Output:** problem statement, evidence, agency-restoring action, remedy, and escalation boundary.

**Avoid:** outrage farming, threats, dehumanization, revenge framing, false accusations, or forced apologies as conversion tactics.

**Metrics:** resolution, recovered trust, repeat contact, escalation, sentiment, and post-resolution retention.

## 7. Acedia — friction and action initiation

**Disciplines:** present bias, choice architecture, cognitive load, implementation intentions, goal-gradient effects, human-computer interaction.

**Use when:** customers agree with the value but delay, abandon, become overloaded, or fail to reach first value.

**Algorithm:**

1. Locate the exact drop-off and classify friction: decision, effort, uncertainty, delay, coordination, or accessibility.
2. Choose the smallest meaningful next action that advances the customer's own goal.
3. Reduce simultaneous choices; reveal complexity progressively.
4. Use a transparent, reversible default only when it reflects likely user welfare.
5. Convert intent into a concrete cue-action plan and provide immediate progress feedback.
6. Preserve undo, skip, save-and-return, and accessible alternatives.
7. Test reduced friction against the current flow while monitoring regret and accidental action.

**Output:** friction diagnosis, minimum action, choice architecture, feedback, and recovery path.

**Avoid:** hidden defaults, obstruction, confirmshaming, forced continuity, inaccessible shortcuts, or removing informed consent.

**Metrics:** activation, time to first value, completion, undo, accidental action, regret, and support contacts.
